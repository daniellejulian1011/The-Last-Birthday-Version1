# CANDLEWICK’S: THE RECORD THAT DID NOT BURN

## THE PLACE

Candlewick’s Celebration Palace opened in 1958 on the edge of a small American town. Its expensive facade promised a complete birthday: tickets, games, cake, photographs, and Mr. Morrow’s stage show. The venue combined an arcade, a birthday auditorium, a curtained performance stage, and back-of-house workshops. The east passage connected the stage to the emergency exit; a low drain ran underneath the foundations.

By 1973 the building was maintained for appearances. The public rooms received new bunting while the workshop accumulated written warnings. Edwin Candlewick regarded lost tickets and refunds as the real emergencies. The damaged stage light bar drew more current than the old cable could handle.

## OCTOBER 27, 1973

Robin Mercer’s seventh birthday was Party Seven. June, fourteen, was the adult-listed supervisor on the venue’s seating sheet, although she was still a child herself. Ruth Hale was the party hostess. Ellis Ward, a nineteen-year-old apprentice, had reported the overheating cable.

The overloaded stage light bar started the fire. Ruth tried the east exit and found the chain Edwin had ordered installed for loss prevention. Smoke reached the birthday tables. Seven people died. Robin was positively identified among the dead. Three other children’s attendance and departures were never reconciled against the original register.

Robin’s death is definitive. The mystery concerns the hidden cause, the missing names in the company’s paperwork, and the unreconciled attendance of other children. It never proposes that Robin survived.

## THE COVER-UP

Edwin retained the originals and submitted an amended guest list to the insurer. Party Seven was removed. Ruth’s November 4 sworn statement described the chain and Edwin taking the register; it was suppressed with the records. Robin’s certificate was omitted from the company’s submission despite the family’s positive identification.

The public story became an unforeseeable electrical accident. The building never reopened. Its owner left the rooms substantially untouched while retaining ownership of the site. The original exit order, cable warning, recorder, guest register, and witness statement remained behind a locked archive door.

## OCTOBER 14, 1983

June is twenty-four, a freelance photographer. A demolition notice reaches her letterbox. Clearance begins the next morning, October 15, thirteen days before what would have been Robin’s seventeenth birthday. She returns to collect a record that can outlive the building.

Ellis, now twenty-nine and a county electrician, is at the service gate with a radio. He wants his old warning to count. Nora Bell, thirty-two, waits beyond the property with a running car and a way to publish copies. Edwin, sixty-one, contacts June on the office telephone and offers money for the originals. Ruth never physically appears in the 1983 story; her presence is a dated adult recording and June’s memory.

## MR. MORROW

During the operating years, Mr. Morrow was a cheerful ivory-and-pink birthday rabbit. A performer and prerecorded greetings gave him a familiar voice. He had clean gloves, an oversized painted face, and a mechanical smile designed to be visible from the last row.

In 1983 the costume is ruined: hollow eyes, scorched cloth, bent ears, an exposed armature, stained gloves, and heavy shoes. He is completely mute. He does not whisper, imitate anyone, sing, speak through a radio, or deliver dialogue. His presence is revealed by weight on the floor, a silhouette moving between cabinets, a glove on glass, and the absence of the figure expected in a photograph.

The severed performance cable provides a concrete impossibility: his movement cannot be explained by the stage’s original drive assembly. The story leaves the agency inside the costume unresolved. It does not identify him as Robin or offer Robin’s survival as a revelation.

## THE PLAYER’S AGENCY

June can refuse to enter, take partial proof out, preserve the complete record, sell her silence, burn the palace, sacrifice herself for the documents, become trapped beneath the floor, or be caught. The complete record requires the exit-order ledger, Ruth’s testimony, the original guest list, Robin’s certificate, and an escape shared with Ellis.

Helping Ellis matters because the story is about a warning left unheard. Preserving the documents matters because a correct suspicion cannot replace evidence. The blue ribbon offers an intimate truth; the signed ledger offers a public one. Neither substitutes for the other.

## CHARACTER DIRECTION

**June:** Practical rather than fearless. Her short observations give the player room to see the scene. Anger surfaces when the company’s wording turns Robin into a missing row.

**Edwin:** Threatening through composure. He uses “incident,” “private property,” and “settlement” to keep people inside the language of his business.

**Ellis:** Concrete instructions, brief admissions. His guilt never makes the player responsible for comforting him; it motivates him to hold the exit.

**Nora:** Wants a provable story and physical copies. Her final question distinguishes the full record from a survivor’s incomplete testimony.

**Ruth:** A witness whose adult archival line asks to be recorded accurately. She is not a present-day apparition.

**Robin:** An ordinary seven-year-old preserved through a seating plan, a ribbon, and a family memory. He has no 1983 voice or playable role.
